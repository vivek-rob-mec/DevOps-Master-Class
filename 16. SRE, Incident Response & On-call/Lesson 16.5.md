# Module 16 — SRE, Incident Response & On-call

## Lesson 5: Symptom-based Alerting and Healthy On-call

# 16.5.1 Page on urgent action

```text
page   → user impact or imminent objective breach; act now
ticket → important work within business cadence
log    → evidence, trend, or automation input
```

Pages should be symptoms whenever possible: availability, latency, correctness, freshness, or error-budget burn. CPU, memory, queue length, and disk are valuable causes but page only when they require immediate intervention.

# 16.5.2 On-call system

```text
primary → acknowledges and leads initial response
secondary → backup/escalation and parallel investigation
incident commander → coordination for declared incidents
service/domain experts → scoped expertise
communications → stakeholders and status
```

The schedule must account for time zones, leave, handoffs, escalation, access, and maximum sustainable load. Being on-call without training, authority, tools, or rest is organizational failure.

# 16.5.3 Readiness checklist

```text
□ production access tested before shift
□ laptop/network/pager path tested
□ service map, dashboards, SLOs, runbooks available
□ recent changes and known risks handed over
□ escalation contacts current
□ maintenance and silence calendar reviewed
□ shadow shifts completed
```

Google's on-call guidance connects paging to explicit SLOs and treats excessive alerts as an engineering problem rather than something responders should absorb. ([Google SRE][1])

# 16.5.4 Alert quality review

For every page record actionable, valid, unique, and user-impacting. Review false positives, repeated pages, pages with no action, missed incidents, time to acknowledge, night load, and escalations. Delete or redesign bad alerts; do not merely increase thresholds.

# 16.5.5 Lab

Run a one-hour simulation with a primary, secondary, and observer. Inject one SLO burn, one harmless CPU spike, one dependency failure, and one duplicate alert storm. Grade detection, ownership, escalation, cognitive load, and safe recovery.

# 16.5.6 Interview answer

> **A page means a human must act urgently to protect a user objective. I use symptom and SLO-burn alerting, route by ownership and severity, maintain primary/secondary escalation, test access and runbooks, and review every page for actionability. Pager load, fatigue, and recovery time are system health metrics.**

# 16.5.7 Beginner mental model: a smoke alarm, not a sensor display

A smoke alarm wakes a person because urgent action may prevent harm. It does not sound for every normal temperature change. Monitoring can record thousands of conditions; paging reserves human attention for urgent, actionable risk.

An alert should answer:

```text
What user/objective is at risk?
Why must someone act now?
Who owns the response?
What safe first actions and evidence exist?
How will the responder know recovery is complete?
```

# 16.5.8 Symptom versus cause

```text
symptom: checkout availability is burning its budget rapidly
cause candidate: database connections are saturated
```

Page on the symptom because causes change and not every cause signal produces user impact. Put cause panels in the dashboard/runbook, and page on an imminent resource condition only when delay would remove the ability to prevent harm.

# 16.5.9 Alert lifecycle

```text
signal/query -> pending -> firing -> routed -> grouped/inhibited
-> notification attempt -> human acknowledgement -> incident/action
-> resolved -> reviewed
```

Measure every stage. A firing rule is not proof that a person received or understood a page.

# 16.5.10 Alert content contract

A good page includes:

```yaml
summary: "Checkout availability burning 14x in production"
impact: "Customers may fail to submit orders"
service: checkout-api
environment: production
owner: team-checkout
severity: page
started_at: generated by alert system
dashboard: stable-link
runbook: stable-link
recent_change: deployment-link-if-known
```

Annotations must render with real alert labels. Test for missing variables, dead links, and huge cardinality that creates one notification per pod.

# 16.5.11 On-call rotation design

Define:

```text
primary and secondary coverage
shift start/end and time-zone fairness
handoff overlap and known-risk summary
leave/illness substitution
escalation timeouts and management path
maximum consecutive nights/weekends
compensation and recovery time
training/shadow/certification for access
```

A rotation with too few qualified people is an organizational reliability risk. Track it openly rather than depending on heroism.

# 16.5.12 Access and authority before the shift

Responders must safely test:

- pager receipt and acknowledgement;
- laptop, VPN/identity, cluster/cloud/log access;
- break-glass process without actually exposing credentials;
- deployment abort/revert and traffic controls;
- current service catalog, dependencies, dashboards, and runbooks;
- escalation contacts and incident declaration.

Access requested during an outage lengthens recovery and pressures responders into unsafe workarounds.

# 16.5.13 Cognitive load and fatigue

Track distribution, not only averages:

```text
pages per shift and per person
night pages and sleep interruptions
duplicate/non-actionable pages
acknowledgement and engagement time
incidents and escalations
hours spent after shift
handoff quality and missed signals
```

After a difficult night, arrange recovery time and redistribute work. Fatigue increases change and diagnosis risk.

# 16.5.14 Alert quality scorecard

For each page, ask:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Valid | Did the condition actually occur? |
| Relevant | Did it threaten a defined user/service objective? |
| Actionable | Could the responder do something now? |
| Unique | Was this a useful incident signal rather than a duplicate? |
| Timely | Was there enough time to reduce harm? |
| Clear | Did ownership, impact, and runbook make sense? |

Assign an owner and deadline to noisy-page remediation just like a product defect.

# 16.5.15 Real hands-on: design three alert classes

For `todo-api`, implement and test:

1. **Page:** fast SLO burn affecting create-todo journey.
2. **Ticket:** disk forecast reaches capacity inside planned provisioning lead time.
3. **Dashboard-only:** CPU increase with no saturation or SLI impact.

For each, document expression, labels, pending window, no-data behavior, route, receiver, runbook, and resolution proof. Use rule unit tests plus a clearly marked synthetic delivery test.

# 16.5.16 Duplicate-storm exercise

Generate fifty pod-level cause alerts for one service outage. Observe routing/grouping and responder cognition. Redesign to:

```text
one service symptom page
grouped supporting context
inhibition of dependent alerts
pod-level evidence retained on dashboard
```

Do not suppress evidence; change how it interrupts people.

# 16.5.17 Missed-page exercise

Break a synthetic receiver credential. Follow the rule-to-human path, repair it through the secret source, and verify actual receipt. Define backup escalation if the primary provider or corporate identity system fails.

Monitor a canary page with care: test notifications must not create alarm fatigue or be confused with real incidents.

# 16.5.18 Healthy handoff template

```markdown
## Current service/SLO state
## Active incidents and mitigations
## Recent/high-risk deployments
## Maintenance and planned silences
## Capacity or dependency risks
## Temporary access/configuration
## Expected pages and escalation changes
## Actions due during next shift
```

Both people confirm the handoff. A chat message without acknowledgement is not reliable transfer of duty.

# 16.5.19 Escalation and psychological safety

Escalate when impact or uncertainty exceeds the responder's authority/skill, when the runbook says so, or when safe time thresholds pass. Early escalation is good risk management.

Do not punish responders for asking for help. Protect them from abusive stakeholders, ambiguous executive direction, and pressure to make undocumented risky changes. The incident commander owns coordination during major events.

# 16.5.20 On-call readiness review

- Rotation has sufficient trained people and fair coverage.
- Primary, secondary, incident, and management escalation are current.
- Access and notification are tested before every rotation period.
- SLO pages are actionable and grouped by service/journey.
- No-data and alert-delivery failures are observed.
- Runbooks contain verified, safe, low-dependency actions.
- Page load and fatigue drive engineering prioritization.
- Handoffs and post-shift recovery are explicit.
- Service owners share responsibility for their production behavior.

# 16.5.21 Certification and interview preparation

Healthy on-call and alert engineering are professional SRE competencies even where a certification focuses more on tools. Verify specific exam objectives separately.

**Beginner: What should page a human?**  Urgent user impact or imminent objective breach that requires action now.

**Intermediate: Why not page on every high CPU event?**  CPU is a cause signal; without saturation or user risk it may be normal. Keep it diagnostic or create a preventive ticket based on forecast.

**Intermediate: What makes an alert actionable?**  Clear impact, owner, urgency, scoped evidence, verified runbook/links, and an available mitigation or escalation.

**Senior: How do you reduce alert fatigue?**  Review page outcomes, page on symptoms, aggregate/group/inhibit duplicates, repair rules and ownership, and reduce the underlying failures.

**Senior: What is a healthy on-call rotation?**  Sufficient trained staffing, fair shifts, primary/secondary escalation, tested access/tools, manageable page load, good handoff, authority, and recovery time.

**Expert: Alert fired but no person received it—what do you investigate?**  Rule state, send, route, silence/inhibition, receiver response, provider delivery, escalation, and device/human receipt; add an external canary.

**Architect: How do you measure on-call sustainability?**  Distribution of pages/night interruptions, actionability, incidents, escalations, work hours, staffing/cognitive load, and engineering time spent eliminating causes.

**Never-forget answer:** a page is an expensive request for immediate human judgment. Protect that channel with user symptoms, clear ownership, tested delivery, and sustainable staffing.
